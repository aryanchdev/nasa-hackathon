
Software needs -

- Keep it fast and simple: Astronauts do not have time to fill out 20-question forms every morning.  
    
- ==🔴Zero typing (Hands free design) - In space, pressing a keyboard key pushes your body backward. Developers should use minimum physical touches like single-tap body maps, voice logging and camera scans.==  
    
- Link daily habits to health: Remind them to drink water and eat enough, log sleep hours and mood, and track core vitals (heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, blood sugar) which we will talk about in detail 
    
- Easy medical help: Astronauts are not doctors and on deep-space missions they cannot quickly call Earth. Clicking a body part should explain why it hurts in space and give simple steps to fix it.  
    

Daily Life & Environment  

- Sleeping pods: Astronauts sleep strapped inside tiny, closet-sized cabins so they do not float around and bump into equipment.  
    
- No running water: They wash with wet soapy cloths, use rinse-free shampoo, and spit toothpaste into a tissue because floating water bubbles can ruin electronics.  
    
- Strict schedules: The crew eats from pouches, talks to Mission Control, and follows a tight minute-by-minute daily work plan.  
    
- Mandatory workouts: Every single astronaut must exercise 2 to 2.5 hours a day just to stay healthy.  
    
- Poor sleep (6 hours average): They get an 8.5-hour sleep window, but loud cooling fans and seeing 16 sunrises a day (or total darkness on the way to Mars) confuse their body clock.  
    
- Hiding symptoms: Astronauts are proud, high-performing professionals. They often hide minor pain or stress so doctors on Earth do not pull them off an upcoming spacewalk.  
    

Diet, Eating & Water  

- Pouch meals: Food comes freeze-dried or pre-cooked in plastic pouches. Fresh fruit like apples or oranges is a rare treat that only lasts two weeks after a supply rocket visits.  
    
- Why food tastes bland: Without gravity, fluid pools in their head and clogs their sinuses like a head cold. Because smell and taste are linked, food tastes bland and they crave hot sauce and spices.  
    
- The "No Crumbs" rule: Bread is banned because floating crumbs get stuck in eyes or air filters. They use flour tortillas instead and use liquid droppers for salt and pepper.  
    
- Undereating (Menu fatigue): Getting tired of eating the same pouches alongside a floating stomach causes astronauts to eat 20% to 30% fewer calories, leading to muscle and bone loss.  
    
- Forgetting to drink water: In space, blood stays in the chest, which tricks the brain into thinking the body is already full of water. Astronauts rarely feel thirsty and must force themselves to drink from straw pouches.  
    
- Missing nutrients: Spacecraft windows block sunlight, so astronauts must take Vitamin D pills. They also have to watch salt intake, because high-sodium pouch food speeds up bone loss.  
    

Everyday Physical Problems (How Their Body Feels)  

- Puffy face and blurry vision: Fluid shifting up to the head makes their face puffy, causes headaches, and presses on the back of their eyes. Over months, this pressure flattens the eyeball and makes reading screens blurry.  
    
- Lower back pain: Without gravity pressing down, an astronaut's spine stretches out and they grow up to 2 inches taller. This pulls on back muscles and gives them a dull, constant backache.  
    
- Bone loss and kidney stones: Because floating takes zero effort, bones lose strength every week. That extra bone calcium washes out through the kidneys, which easily causes kidney stones if they do not drink enough water.  
    
- Sore hands and feet: The bottoms of their feet get baby-soft while the tops get calloused from hooking into foot loops. During spacewalks, stiff pressurized gloves bruise their fingers and fingernails.  
    

Some Other Points -

- Stuffy air pockets (Morning headaches): On Earth, warm breath rises. In space, air stays still. If an air fan is weak, the carbon dioxide an astronaut breathes out forms an invisible bubble around their head while sleeping, causing morning headaches and brain fog.  
    
- The radio delay problem: Close to Earth, astronauts can talk to a doctor live. On a Mars mission, radio messages take up to 20 minutes each way. If someone gets hurt or sick, our software has to guide the crew right away without waiting on Earth.
